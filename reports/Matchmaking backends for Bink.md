# Score mutual fit, cap exposure, log real games

Tinder, Hinge, Facebook Marketplace and, as far as anyone can tell, Bumble all run the same three-step pipeline. They apply hard eligibility filters, score the survivors on predicted engagement, then re-rank for balance, integrity and diversity. The machine learning and infrastructure around that pipeline (geosharded Elasticsearch, two-tower embeddings, GPU rankers) exist only because these apps serve tens of millions of users. Bink, with one city and hundreds to low thousands of players, can collapse the whole pipeline into one Postgres function. The ideas worth copying are not the models. The first is to **optimise for the mutual outcome rather than one-way appeal**: Hinge's 2025 switch to a model predicting mutual compatibility lifted matches and contact exchanges by more than 15%, and a 2026 field study found that ranking by predicted dates instead of predicted likes roughly tripled dates. The second is to **ration attention**: like caps, reply-before-you-match rules and exposure caps stop a few popular people from being flooded. The third is to **log the deeper success events**, such as replies, real meetings and "would do it again", from day one so the hand-set weights can be learned later. Sports partner apps and rating systems add what dating apps lack: a skill rating that carries an explicit uncertainty, self-ratings treated only as a starting guess, and results confirmed by both sides. For Bink, the practical result is a security-invoker `for_you()` RPC that scores skill fit, shared courts, overlapping play times and play style, wrapped in request caps, 72-hour expiry, exposure damping and an interaction log. Collaborative filtering, embeddings and any hidden "desirability" score should wait or be skipped entirely.

## Four giants, one funnel: filter, score, re-rank

Tinder has published the most about its retrieval. Elasticsearch narrows tens of millions of profiles to **about 1,000 candidates** using hard filters (age, gender, distance), an embedded LightGBM scorer, and a Roaring-bitmap filter that drops the roughly 10,000 profiles a typical user has already swiped (up to 70,000 for heavy users). Deep-learning models on GPUs then rank those candidates, and a reranker "balance[s]" the results ([Elastic](https://www.elastic.co/customers/tinder)). Tinder's public list of ranking inputs names **activity as "the most important factor"**, especially being "active at the same time". After that come proximity, stated preferences, interests, photo similarity to profiles you liked, and Like/Nope rates on you *and on profiles in your area* ([Tinder Press Room](https://www.tinderpressroom.com/powering-tinder-r-the-method-behind-our-matching/)). Tinder said in 2019 that Elo is "old news", but critics noted that the replacement still depends on who swipes right on you ([Engadget](https://www.engadget.com/2019-03-18-tinder-dumps-desirability-scores.html)). The "Likes and Nopes in your area" input looks like a normalised popularity score under a new name.

Facebook Marketplace has the clearest official description of the same funnel. Its system card says inventory is gathered from recency and relevance sources, with policy-rejected listings excluded, and "around 1,000 listings" go on to **eight separate prediction models**: hide, click, save, share, swipe photos, view the seller's profile, start a conversation, and "communicate multiple times". Listings are then sorted by a single value score ([Meta Transparency Center](https://transparency.meta.com/features/explaining-ranking/fb-marketplace/)). Meta's Instagram Explore write-up gives the general form of that score: **Expected Value = W_click·P(click) + W_like·P(like) − W_see_less·P(see less)**. A final stage applies integrity and diversity rules such as "do not show items from the same authors in a sequence" ([Engineering at Meta](https://engineering.fb.com/2023/08/09/ml-applications/scaling-instagram-explore-recommendations-system/)). Marketplace treats distance as a hard radius gate *and* as a learned feature: "distance to the buyer" is an input to the save model. Users complain that a straight-line radius returns results that "would make me cross a body of water" ([AOL](https://www.aol.com/facebook-marketplace-needs-stop-showing-094501206.html)). That complaint applies directly to Victoria's harbours and inlets.

Hinge discloses less, but in a more useful direction. It has said since 2018 that its matching is "based on a variation of the Gale-Shapley algorithm" combined with machine learning ([Fortune](https://fortune.com/2024/01/18/hinge-ceo-justin-mcleod-interview-attractiveness-score-algorithm-rose-jail/)). Gale-Shapley is the stable-matching method that pairs people by having them "propose" down ranked lists. Hinge runs it on preference lists inferred from likes and passes, in a single pool (the "stable roommates" variant), to produce one reciprocal "Most Compatible" pick a day. Early tests showed **8x** more phone-number exchanges than with other recommendations ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/?p=1668547)). Bumble has published almost nothing about how it ranks. The only confirmed rules are closest-first ordering, automatic radius expansion when you "run out of people to swipe on" ([Bumble BFF help](https://bumble.com/en/bff/help/為什麼我會看到位於其他地區的人)), and one-way filters that shape only your own deck ([Bumble Support](https://support.bumble.com/hc/en-us/articles/28423691289629)). Its executives call verification data "the high-grade fuel" for a next-generation engine whose design they have not disclosed ([Motley Fool](https://www.fool.com/earnings/call-transcripts/2025/11/06/bumble-bmbl-q3-2025-earnings-call-transcript)).

| | Candidate generation | Scoring | Re-rank / controls | What Bink keeps |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Tinder | Geosharded Elasticsearch, hard filters, already-swiped exclusion, ~1,000 out | Deep models on GPUs; activity, proximity, feedback, interests | "Balancing" reranker; like caps | Same-time activity, the exclusion anti-join, caps |
| Hinge | Mutual dealbreakers as hard filters | Deep model predicting *mutual* compatibility (2025) | Daily reciprocal pick; Your Turn Limits; Roses | Mutual objective, capacity limits, post-meeting check-in |
| Bumble | Nearest first, radius expands when supply runs out | Undisclosed; profile quality and verification as "fuel" | Match expiry timer; verified-only filter | Constraint relaxation, one-way filters, expiry, verification badge |
| Marketplace | Recency and relevance sources, policy filter, ~1,000 out | Per-action probabilities combined into a value score | Integrity demotion, diversity | Value-sum score shape, logging several outcomes, layered trust |

Each of these companies separates eligibility, scoring and re-ranking, and none of them publishes its weights. Bink should copy that separation. It should not copy the infrastructure, which exists to bring an enormous candidate pool down to a scorable size. With fewer than about 5,000 players, Bink can score every eligible candidate on every request.

## Mutual outcomes beat one-way appeal

The strongest evidence in this research concerns *what* to predict. Hinge's 2025 recommender "uses deep learning to deliver more thoughtful recommendations by better predicting mutual compatibility" ([Hinge newsroom](https://hinge.co/newsroom/hinge-2025-product-evolution)). Since its global launch in March 2025 it has driven **a greater than 15% increase in matches and contact exchanges** ([Match Group Q1 2025, SEC](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0000891103/000089110325000074/mtch8-k20250508ex992.htm)). Hinge's CEO also says its "taste profile" weights *who you like* and *who likes you back* equally, and that "we don't really have an attractiveness score" ([Fortune](https://fortune.com/2024/01/18/hinge-ceo-justin-mcleod-interview-attractiveness-score-algorithm-rose-jail/)).

A 2026 production study on CoupLink, a Japanese dating service, puts numbers on this. Ranking candidates by predicted **like rate** produced 0.0401 dates per user. Ranking by predicted **dating rate**, the product of both users' login, like and reciprocal-like probabilities, produced **0.1231**, even though it generated fewer likes (3.693 vs 5.384). Before the intervention, the top 0.1% of receivers got **4.6% of all recommendations**, and their reply rate after a mutual like was about **one-third** of everyone else's. Capping each receiver's expected inbound interest raised receiver-side outcomes. The authors call the classic stability guarantee "not essential" in a recommendation setting ([Sekiya et al., arXiv 2602.19689](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.19689v1)). The study has limits: the field test compared only two regions and lowered raw total dates. Its main message still holds. Optimising for attention concentrates it on a few people, who then stop responding.

Marketplace reaches the same conclusion from commerce. "Start a conversation" and "communicate multiple times" are separate prediction targets ([Meta Transparency Center](https://transparency.meta.com/features/explaining-ranking/fb-marketplace/)), so a back-and-forth exchange counts as a stronger success than a first message. Meta's Que2Engage paper adds a warning about training data. Models trained only on engaged examples learn nothing about candidates that are relevant but unappealing, such as a listing with an unreasonable price. The fix was to add **impressions that were shown but got no engagement as hard negatives**, which raised engagement ROC-AUC from 55.88 to 76.90 ([Que2Engage, arXiv 2302.11052](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2302.11052)). For Bink, a player who keeps scrolling past 4.5-rated strangers at a court across town is giving exactly that kind of signal, and the impression log has to capture it.

Hinge's "We Met" feature shows how to measure what actually matters. A few days after two matches exchange numbers, Hinge asks privately whether they met and whether they would go on a second date. It says it uses the answers "to provide better recommendations in the future" ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2018/10/16/hinge-is-first-dating-app-to-actually-measure-real-world-success)). Bink's equivalent is easier to collect, because a game either happened or it didn't, and the existing `hill_matches` two-sided confirmation already records part of it. A private "play again?" prompt after a confirmed game gives the best label Bink can get for whether a pairing worked.

The objective also determines what Bink should *not* build. Tinder's dropped Elo score, a single hidden desirability number computed from swipe votes ([The Next Web](https://thenextweb.com/news/tinder-is-secretly-scoring-your-desirability-and-picking-matches-that-are-in-your-league)), caused a reputational backlash and is the opposite of mutual fit. Pickleball already has an honest, explicit measure of how good someone is, their skill rating, so Bink has no reason to infer popularity and rank on it.

## Scarcity rules spread attention around

Each dating app limits attention on purpose, and the published results are among the few hard numbers in this research. Tinder's 2015 right-swipe cap targeted people who "only swipe right just to see who likes them back". Within ten days, **matches per right swipe rose 25%, messages per match rose 25%, and spam bots fell by more than half** ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2015/03/12/hate-it-or-love-it-tinders-right-swipe-limit-is-working)). Hinge's Your Turn Limits stops users with too many unanswered matches from connecting with anyone new, and **raised responsiveness by 20%** in testing ([Hinge press](https://hinge.co/press/your-turn-limits)). Hinge says scarce Roses are "two times more effective at actually getting you on a date", because "if everyone had unlimited Roses, then that Rose wouldn't mean anything anymore" ([Fortune](https://fortune.com/2024/01/18/hinge-ceo-justin-mcleod-interview-attractiveness-score-algorithm-rose-jail/)). Bumble's match-expiry timer was 24 hours for a decade. In August 2026 Bumble lengthened it to **72 hours**, and more than half of surveyed members said the longer window improved their experience ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/08/11/bumble-ditches-its-rule-that-kept-men-from-making-the-first-move/)).

Not every scarcity mechanic is honest. Tinder claims a Super Like makes a match 3x more likely, but an analysis of more than 7,000 user data exports found heavy Super Like senders matched at 1.94% against a 2.04% median ([Swipestats](https://www.swipestats.io/blog/super-like-on-tinder)). Paid Boosts, which make you "1 of the top profiles in your area for 30 minutes" ([Tinder Help](https://www.help.tinder.com/hc/en-us/articles/115004506186)), are a way to make money, not a matching signal. The pattern Bink should borrow is a *limit* that makes each invite mean something, not paid placement. Pickleball also has a natural capacity limit, since a person can only play so many games a week. A daily cap on invites to strangers, a "reply first" rule once someone has too many pending invites, an expiry window of about 72 hours, and perhaps one free "priority invite" a week together cover what like caps, Your Turn Limits, the Bumble timer and Roses each do. None of them needs a payment system.

Bumble BFF offers one more lesson. Friend matching does not need the hidden double opt-in that Tinder patented ([USPTO Gazette](https://patentsgazette.uspto.gov/week40/OG/html/1527-1/US12105941-20241001.html)). Since moving BFF onto the Geneva group platform, Bumble reports higher retention, and **group joins nearly doubled** between December 2025 and March 2026 ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/05/05/bumbles-paying-users-are-slipping-as-it-bets-on-an-overhaul-later-this-year/)). Bink's "request to play" fits better as a visible, directed invite with accept or decline, like Bumble's single message that waits for a reply. A blind mutual like adds friction without adding much safety, because the `open_to_play` flag and invite caps already protect the person receiving invites.

## Racket-sport ratings supply the missing confidence term

Partner apps all use the same filter set. Hitch filters on distance, skill, availability, gender and format ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/app/id6670320911)). PickleMatchApp adds age and **favourite courts** ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/picklematchapp/id6749190103)). None of them publishes a ranking algorithm, and none of the apps reviewed makes "who plays at this court" the main screen, which leaves Bink's court-first design unclaimed. Playtomic contributes two mechanics worth copying. Players outside an open match's level range can "request a place" instead of being blocked ([Playtomic](https://helpmanager.playtomic.com/hc/en-gb/articles/20535718052241-Request-a-spot-in-an-Open-Match)). Friendly and competitive matches are kept separate, and only competitive ones change your level ([Playtomic](https://helpmanager.playtomic.com/hc/en-gb/articles/20535188135185-Friendly-vs-Competitive-Open-Matches)). That split maps directly onto Bink's `fun / social / competitive` play styles.

Every serious rating system pairs a skill estimate with a confidence figure. DUPR rates players from 2.000 to 8.000, sets **team rating as the average of the two players**, uses points scored against expectation rather than just wins, and shows a **Reliability Score** from 1 to 100% ([DUPR](https://dupr.com/how-it-works)). UTR shows a "projected" rating until a player has **about 5 results** ([UTR](https://support.universaltennis.com/en/support/solutions/articles/9000151963)) and weights recent matches against reliable opponents more heavily ([UTR](https://support.universaltennis.com/en/support/solutions/articles/9000233354)). TrueSkill models skill as a mean μ and an uncertainty σ, and **sums teammates' skills**. It defines a good match as a high draw probability, and high uncertainty lowers match quality even when the means are equal. It needs about **12 games** to pin down a head-to-head player, and warns that real numbers "can be up to three times higher" ([Microsoft Research](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/project/trueskill-ranking-system/)). Playtomic starts new players from a questionnaire and moves low-reliability players' levels further per match ([Playtomic](https://playerhelp.playtomic.com/hc/en-gb/articles/19831827459345-The-Playtomic-Levels-ups-downs)).

This confidence term solves Bink's self-rating problem. Community writers describe rated open play drifting until "4.5" means "anyone confident enough to sign up" ([Pickleball Union](https://pickleballunion.com/how-true-is-rated-open-play-in-pickleball/)). DUPR notes that sandbagging is more common where organisers rely on self-ratings ([DUPR FAQ](https://www.dupr.com/vi/post/upa-integration-and-dupr-algorithm-faqs---all-you-need-to-know)). The established pattern treats the self-rating as a **starting guess with high uncertainty**, labels it as such, and lets confirmed results replace it. In Bink's score, a self-rated player should get a *wider* skill tolerance than a DUPR-linked one, because a self-rated 3.5 really means "somewhere between 3.0 and 4.0".

Ladder practice covers the king of the hill. Challenge ladders give a deadline to accept, and an unanswered challenge becomes a default loss ([Luna Sol ladder rules](https://sites.google.com/view/lunasolsportspbladder/challenge-ladder-rules)). One league verifies with "1 player per side" and a **3-hour auto-confirm** ([league guide](https://files.trackie.com/uploads/redactor/2026-05-18-16-30-14-322499808-1081.pdf)). Bink's current rule, that a result counts only once both players confirm, will leave results stuck when the loser never confirms. Penalties also need care. Playtomic users report being wrongly marked as no-shows and charged ([Trustpilot](https://ca.trustpilot.com/review/www.playtomic.io)), so reliability should affect ranking privately rather than show up as a public punishment. One gap in Bink's spec: `hill_matches` records a single winner and loser, but most recreational pickleball is doubles. Both DUPR and TrueSkill rate individuals within teams, and Bink's table will need team columns before streaks or ratings can handle doubles.

## Courts solve cold start better than any ranking

None of these companies publishes how it handles a small, thin market, and that is Bink's hardest problem. Bumble's only documented answer is to relax the radius when supply runs out. Bink's version is to relax constraints in order: widen the skill band, then accept nearby courts, then accept non-overlapping times. Each relaxed result gets a label ("slightly above your level", "plays 10 minutes away"), so the directory is never empty. Bumble's one-way filters ([Bumble Support](https://support.bumble.com/hc/en-us/articles/28423691289629)) also help a thin pool: your preferences should shape what *you* see, not hide you from others. Marketplace's click model includes a **"click-through rate upper confidence bound"** ([Meta Transparency Center](https://transparency.meta.com/features/explaining-ranking/fb-marketplace/)). This is an exploration bonus that gives items with few impressions the benefit of the doubt, and it carries over directly to new players. Tinder and Bumble have never confirmed a "new user boost", so Bink adopting one is its own design choice, not an industry standard.

The larger point is that courts already exist on day one, before any players do. Pickleheads seeded more than 16,000 court listings before it needed players at them ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/ca/app/id6448714446)). Playtomic turns a club's empty slots into joinable open matches automatically ([Playtomic](https://helpmanager.playtomic.com/hc/en-gb/articles/20535035123473-How-to-configure-Open-Matches-at-your-Club)). Victoria already gathers strangers at fixed places and times: drop-in courts at Topaz Park and Beacon Hill ([City of Victoria](https://www.victoria.ca/node/3176)), VRPA drop-ins at CARSA, Cordova Bay and Juan de Fuca ([VRPA](https://secure.pickleballcanada.org/club/victoria-regional-pickleball-association/1033/where-to-play/7652/)), and a Meetup group at Beacon Hill on Mon/Wed/Fri ([Meetup](https://www.meetup.com/victoria-pickleball-and-social-club/events/tgbsrtyhckbfc)). A court page becomes useful once it has a handful of regulars, while a citywide "for you" list needs far more players before it is worth opening. Shared home courts are also Bink's strongest ranking signal, playing the same role as mutual friends in Facebook's People You May Know ([Meta Transparency Center](https://transparency.meta.com/features/explaining-ranking/fb-people-you-may-know/)). They also capture travel distance across water better than a straight-line radius does.

Location privacy follows from the same design. If player location is derived from public court locations plus a coarse area, Bink never needs to store player GPS. Even so, the approved spec lets any signed-in user read `player_courts`, and a home court combined with usual play times tells strangers where and when someone can be found. Showing exact court links only to connections, and only a court count to everyone else, is worth deciding before launch.

## Prioritised recommendations for Bink's matching backend

The Supabase-specific choices below follow documented platform guidance. Supabase says to prefer `security invoker` functions because a definer function "can return rows the caller isn't allowed to read". Definer helpers belong in an unexposed `private` schema with `search_path = ''`, and `auth.uid()` should be wrapped as `(select auth.uid())` so its result is cached per statement ([Supabase Functions](https://supabase.com/docs/guides/database/functions); [Supabase RLS](https://supabase.com/docs/guides/database/postgres/row-level-security)). Paginate with keyset pagination rather than OFFSET, which also prevents results drifting between pages ([Use The Index, Luke](https://use-the-index-luke.com/no-offset)). Use Broadcast from a database trigger, rather than Postgres Changes, for match notifications ([Supabase Realtime](https://supabase.com/docs/guides/realtime/subscribing-to-database-changes)). The score weights below are starting points chosen by judgment. No source publishes weights for this problem.

| Priority | Build | How, in Supabase | Borrowed from |
|---|---|---|---|
| **P0** | `for_you()` RPC replacing the placeholder order | `language sql stable security invoker`. Hard filters: not self, `open_to_play`, no block either way (via a `private` definer helper), not hidden or snoozed, active in the last N days. Score = weighted sum of skill fit (gap divided by a tolerance that is wider when self-rated, narrower when DUPR-linked), shared home or regular courts, usual-times overlap, play-style compatibility, court-to-court distance and recency. Weights live in a `ranking_weights` table. Round the score and order by `(score desc, id desc)` with keyset paging | Marketplace value model; Tinder signal families; Supabase docs |
| **P0** | Interaction log from day one | `match_events(viewer_id, target_id, kind, surface, created_at)`, where kind is impression, profile view, invite, accept, decline, expire, played or play again. Index on `(viewer_id, created_at)` | Marketplace's multiple outcomes; Que2Engage negatives; Hinge We Met |
| **P0** | Directed play requests with expiry and caps | `play_requests` with `primary key(from_id,to_id)`, `check(from_id<>to_id)`, status and `expires_at = now()+72h`. Accept writes `connections(a<b)`. A BEFORE INSERT trigger rejects the request beyond a daily cap on new invites, or when the sender has too many unanswered incoming invites. A pg_cron job expires stale rows | Tinder like cap; Hinge Your Turn Limits; Bumble 72h timer |
| **P0** | Blocks, hides and safety exclusion before ranking | `blocks` and `hidden(until)` tables, enforced in RLS on requests and messaging and in `for_you()`. Profile status `active / needs_attention / hidden` for moderation | Marketplace integrity layer; Bumble quality tiers |
| **P1** | Exposure damping and monitoring | Subtract a penalty for each player's pending incoming invites over the last 7 days. Cap how often the same candidate is shown to the same viewer. Add a decaying exploration bonus for players with few impressions. Track the top-10% share of impressions and invites received weekly | Sekiya ECDA; Marketplace CTR-UCB; Instagram diversity rule |
| **P1** | Constraint relaxation with labels | If fewer than N results come back, rerun with a wider skill band, then nearby courts, then any times, tagging each row with why it was included | Bumble radius expansion; Playtomic "request a place" |
| **P1** | Rating with uncertainty, and hill integrity | Store `rating_mu` and `rating_sigma`, seeded from the self-rating (or DUPR) with a high σ. Update on confirmed results using score margin. Show a "self-rated / projected" badge until about 5 confirmed matches. Add doubles team columns to `hill_matches`. Auto-confirm after a window (reduced weight), let a dispute exclude a result from streaks, cap repeat results against the same opponent, and require minimum activity to hold the hill | DUPR, UTR, TrueSkill, Playtomic; ladder rules |
| **P1** | Private "play again?" check-in | Prompt after a confirmed game, never shown to the other player, stored as a `match_events` row | Hinge We Met |
| **P2** | Learned weights | Once a few thousand invite and accept outcomes exist, fit a logistic regression offline to predict "accepted and played", with shown-but-ignored impressions as negatives. Write the fitted weights back to `ranking_weights` | Tinder LightGBM; Que2Engage |
| **P2** | Daily reciprocal "top picks" | A nightly pg_cron job greedily assigns high-mutual-score pairs, capping how many people see each player | Hinge Most Compatible; ECDA greedy |
| **P2** | Co-play graph term | A two-hop join ("played with people you played with") normalised and added as one more score term | PYMK mutual friends |
| **Skip** | Embeddings, ANN indexes, geosharding, Elo-style desirability, paid boosts | Structured features carry the signal at this scale. Exact scans are trivial. Paid placement corrupts the ranking | Scale analysis across all four platforms |

Three risks need tests on the day the database first runs. First, two players requesting each other at the same moment can each miss the other's uncommitted row under Postgres's default isolation. Take an advisory lock on the ordered pair in the trigger, or reconcile mutual requests nightly. Second, a ranking function that accidentally runs as security definer would leak private rows. pgTAP's `is_definer()` assertion can guard against that ([Supabase pgTAP](https://supabase.com/docs/guides/database/extensions/pgtap)). Third, nobody publishes how many interactions a small social-matching app needs before learned weights beat hand-tuned ones. The P2 work should start with an A/B comparison, not a switchover.

## Conclusion

Bink can treat its "for you" ranking as a market-design problem rather than a machine-learning one. The platforms that improved most recently, Hinge with its mutual-compatibility model and the CoupLink experiment, did so by changing *what* they predicted and *how much attention* any one person receives, not by building bigger models. Bink has three structural advantages that dating apps lack: an explicit skill rating, physical courts that bucket players by place and time, and an outcome (a confirmed game) that can be checked rather than inferred. A transparent SQL score that uses all three, wrapped in invite caps, expiry and exposure damping, will likely beat any learned model Bink could train in its first year.

The deferred product question, "what is a good match?", should be answered with data rather than settled in advance. Shipping the interaction log and the "play again?" check-in together with the first hand-weighted score turns every invite into a labelled example, so the weights can later be tuned from real Victoria games instead of guesses. The court-first map is likely to drive early retention more than any ranking, as Bumble's shift toward groups suggests. That makes the directory's first job modest: never be empty, never flood the best players, and record what happens.
